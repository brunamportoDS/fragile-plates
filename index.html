Fragile Plates · What's Cooking, 2026

A food shock doesn't empty the shelves. It empties the wallet.

We screened 13 staples across 180 countries to find whose plate breaks first when one supplier stops.

Team Fragile PlatesSeptember 2026Methods & sources

Every plate of food is the last stop on a supply chain, and some chains have exactly one link. Lesotho, a mountain kingdom of 2.3 million people, buys 100% of its imported maize from South Africa. Maize is nearly half of what a person in Lesotho eats in a day.

So we asked a simple question of the trade records the UN's Food and Agriculture Organization keeps for every country on earth: if the biggest supplier stopped tomorrow, whose plate breaks first?

We call that number plate share. It is easy to compute and easy to rank. Ranking it is where the argument started.

We built a model. It said nobody would go hungry.

The obvious next step was to simulate the shock: take the top supplier away, let the others fill the gap up to the most they had ever shipped, and count the tonnes that could not be replaced. Convert tonnes to calories. Divide by people.

The answer, for every country, was zero. Brazil had spare maize for Mexico. Australia had spare wheat for Egypt. On paper, the ships always come.

Before trusting that, we did the one thing a model can't argue with: we checked it against something that had already happened.

A fragile plate breaks three ways

Egypt paid. It bought 3% less wheat and paid 54% more for it. But when we ran the same before-and-after for our other cases, they didn't follow Egypt's script.

Mexico paid more and bought more. Its maize imports rose 18% through a 36% price rise, and its supplier count fell from 24 to 8. The shock made it lean in harder.

Ghana couldn't pay. Its rice price barely moved in dollars, but the cedi lost half its value in 2022 and the country defaulted that December. Ghana bought 29% less rice. Not because rice got dearer; because Ghana got poorer.

Same fragility, three different endings. What decides the ending is not the crop, the supplier, or the distance. It is whether the country can afford the bill. That is the thing our plate-share number never measured.

So what

We rebuilt the model around price. If the market reprices the way it did for Egypt in 2022, and a country's food budget does not grow to match, this is what goes missing from each person's plate every day:

The solution: grain or money, and a rule for each country

A planner has two instruments. Hold grain, which costs money every single year in silos, interest and spoilage. Or hold a budget line, which costs nothing until the shock arrives and then pays the higher bill. Our first draft sized a reserve with one formula and got the same answer for all three countries, 4.2 months. That is the model read back, not a decision.

So we costed the two instruments against each other. The ratio between a shock-year bill and a year of carrying grain is the break-even interval: a reserve only pays for itself if shocks come more often than that. Give the tool a country and a price shock and it returns both numbers, and the interval.

Bounded: 0 kcal if fully replaced at the same price · 291 kcal if nothing is replaced. Model C: imported calories × (1 − 1 ÷ (1 + shock)).

Carry assumes USD 20 per tonne a year for storage, 5% capital cost and 2% shrinkage on grain valued at the 2019–23 import price. These are typical bulk-grain figures, not FAOSTAT data; halving or doubling storage moves the break-even by a few years, not the conclusion.

Which instrument depends on how the plate broke. A country that paid more needs money. A country that leaned in on a corridor with no alternative needs its own stock. A country that could not pay needs hard currency, not grain. One rule per country, each with a trigger, a size, a yearly cost and an owner.

Egypt
paid more

Trigger: imported wheat above about USD 350 a tonne, the 2017–21 average plus half the 2022 shock, for two quarters running. Action: release a USD 1.7 billion budget line, not the silos. We checked this against the event. Egypt paid USD 1.59 billion, 1.52 billion and 0.82 billion above 2017–21 prices in 2022, 2023 and 2024, so the line covers both peak years with about 5% to spare and over-covers the average year by a quarter, which is the right shape for a contingency. Prices stayed above the trigger for two years, and a 4.2-month reserve would have bridged one of them. Carrying that reserve costs USD 0.14 billion a year and only pays off if a shock like 2022 recurs more than once every 12 years. Sign forward contracts now with the suppliers that stepped up: France, Bulgaria, Romania. Owner: GASC with the Ministry of Supply.

Mexico
leaned in

Trigger: the US share above 90% for two years (it is 94% now), or the maize import price above about USD 270 a tonne, a quarter over its 2017–21 average. Action: a domestic strategic stock of 6 million tonnes, 4.2 months of imports. Here money cannot buy supply: no other supplier has ever shown more than 0.6 points of extra capacity, and the rail and ports from Brazil and Argentina do not exist yet. Cost: about USD 1.6 billion to fill once, USD 0.23 billion a year to carry, against a USD 2.4 billion bill in a shock year. The carry is the insurance premium for a single-corridor supply. Owner: SEGALMEX with SADER.

Ghana
went without

Trigger: quarterly rice imports below 85% of the trailing three-year average, or the cedi down more than 20% in a year. Both happened in 2022–23. Action: a hard-currency import line of USD 0.11 billion a year, the 29% of tonnes Ghana stopped buying valued at base prices, released on the trigger. Not a grain reserve: the price never moved, the constraint was dollars. Rebuild Thailand, 16% of the market before 2023 and 3% after, as the deliberate second supplier. Owner: Bank of Ghana with the Ministry of Food and Agriculture.

Across all three, a year of carrying grain costs about a tenth of a shock-year bill, so a budget line is the cheaper first instrument unless shocks arrive more than once a decade. Grain reserves earn their keep where money cannot buy supply.

We went looking for the country that runs out of food. We found that almost nobody does. The ships come. Then the bill comes. And whether a plate breaks depends on who is holding it.

Methods & sources

  1. Data. FAOSTAT Detailed Trade Matrix (importer-reported tonnes and value, 2015–2024, 52 million rows) and Food Balances 2010– (calories, population, imports and domestic supply; 2019–2023 means). Bulk downloads, All Data Normalized, retrieved September 2026 and verified against the FAOSTAT website.
  2. Plate share = top supplier's share of imports × the commodity's share of dietary energy × imports ÷ domestic supply. Items are grouped by CPC division so a primary crop is never summed with its processed forms; rice alone has seven.
  3. Excluded: re-export hubs (imports above 105% of domestic supply), net exporters, importers under 10,000 t a year, and the 12 countries absent from Food Balances, including Benin, Mali and Togo. Population-weighted rankings also require a 2% plate-share floor.
  4. The shock. Three bounded models: nothing replaced, fully replaced, replaced but repriced. The repricing (+54%) is Egypt's observed 2017–21 vs 2022–24 change, applied as a scenario to the other cases. Each case's own observed change is reported beside it.
  5. The caveat that matters. We measure exposure, not ability to cope. Qatar imports nearly all its rice and can outbid anyone; Ghana cannot. Read every ranking here with income in mind; the Ghana result is what that gap looks like in the data.
  6. Reserve vs budget line. A reserve is sized to the tonnes a fixed budget can no longer buy, annual imports × (1 − 1 ÷ (1 + shock)). Its yearly carry is storage (USD 20/t), capital (5%) and shrinkage (2%) on grain at the 2019–23 import price; these are typical bulk-grain assumptions, not FAOSTAT data. The budget line is shock × the annual import bill. Break-even = budget line ÷ carry. Egypt's budget number is checked against the extra Egypt actually paid above 2017–21 prices in each of 2022–24: USD 1.59, 1.52 and 0.82 billion. Institutional names (GASC, SEGALMEX) should be verified against current bodies.
  7. What we didn't do. Lose the top two suppliers at once, which is how 2022 actually unfolded. Our first, physical-replacement model is described honestly above because the correction is part of the finding.

Sources: FAOSTAT Detailed Trade Matrix · FAOSTAT Food Balances (2010–). Analysis notebook and code available from the team.
